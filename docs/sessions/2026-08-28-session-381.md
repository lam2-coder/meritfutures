### 3. Two readings are refuted by proof, and one of the proofs needs no data

**Reading A, the 30-day total.** The trailing 7 days are a subset of the trailing 30, so
`last_7d <= total_30` on every possible history, so `ratio_bp <= 10000`, so **the alarm can never
fire on any input, ever.** Not a calibration concern: a proof that reading A makes
[INFRA:160](../architecture/INFRA.md)'s pager a decoration.

**Reading B, the mean daily figure.** Steady state sits near `70000` bp, `2.8x` the threshold. The
pager fires on day one and every day after, and silencing it takes a roughly 64 percent collapse in
payouts, so **the alarm is quiet exactly when something is badly wrong.**

**Reading C survives**, sits near `10000` bp in steady state, and reaches `25000` bp when a week's
payouts run two and a half times normal, which is the sentence the constitution already says.

### 4. The trading-day ruling, and the counter-source it had to survive

The ruling does not rest on the corpus owning a calendar. It rests on the ratio being stationary,
which requires the process filling the numerator to advance on the same clock as the window
bounding it. Payout supply is generated by gates counted in trading days, and the snapshot's own
clock is the last closed day, itself a trading day. A calendar window imposes a third clock.

**[GLOSSARY:47](../GLOSSARY.md) is the source that argues hardest against it** and is quoted in
full in the entry rather than summarised, because a payout settles on the rail's clock and a rule
that dropped weekend settlements would understate money that actually left. **The answer separates
two questions the objection folds together**: the calendar is asked only which snapshot first sees
a settlement, never whether one happened. Ruling 4's attribution is what keeps them apart, and it
is also what makes the `7/30` scaling exact rather than 8 percent biased.
