### 3. The ruling, and the number that carries it

The question "may a dated pointer be repaired" has no answer until you know whether it was true on the day it was written, and that is recoverable from `git` and from nowhere else. So it was recovered, for every pointer in the corpus: `git blame -w` the citing line, read the cited file at that commit, compare.

**Of 289 named pointers in dated records whose write commit is recoverable, 169 are still true, 76 drifted after being true, and 42 were FALSE ON THE DAY THEY WERE WRITTEN.** Those 42 sit in entries whose own verdict columns record the claim as checked. **Repairing one does not restore a record; it converts a check that did not check into a check that did.** That is the whole force of the ruling and it is why the second branch is a refusal rather than a permission.

**The other 8,101 pointers in dated records name no subject, so nobody can tell the two cases apart.** Option (a) is therefore not a choice for 96.6 percent of the population, it is a consequence.

### 4. The instance the dispatch named, refuted

[ADR-384](../decisions/ADR-384.md) recorded `ADR-352:30` as a pointer that had **never** carried its subject, *"thirty lines short on the day it was written"*, and named it as owed to whoever ruled on repairability.

`git log --diff-filter=A` names `3303e58c` as the one commit that has ever touched [ADR-352](../decisions/ADR-352.md), and at that commit `absence-claims.mjs` is 1,508 lines with `key: 'event-sink-caller'` at **822**, which is the number cited. The thirty lines arrived afterwards, in two sibling commits of the same day, and by this row's base the subject is at 875.

**The entry read the pointer at its own base rather than at the commit that wrote it**, which is [ADR-377](../decisions/ADR-377.md) section 8's rule meeting its own dual. It is the second time in three waves that a premise handed to a row has not survived being checked.

### 7. Blockers and landmines
