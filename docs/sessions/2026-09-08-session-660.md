### 4. The definition, which was the first deliverable

**A forcing constraint is a claim, made in a section where an entry names work it did not take, that attributes the not-taking to a stated property of the world rather than to the entry's preference.** Four gates, over a **sentence** rather than a line, with inline code masked so that a colon inside `` `scoped-db.ts:2211` `` cannot split a claim:

1. **PLACE.** The heading matches an owed-work PHRASE, not a token.
2. **RELATION.** One of four kinds: **FENCE**, **RULE**, **COST**, **CAPABILITY**, each with its own surface test.
3. **REFERENT.** The sentence names a path, an `ADR-nnn`, a `PR #nnn` or an `RI`/`CI`/`CV`/`SD`/`MZ`/`VG`/`OI`/`LT`/`INV` identifier.
4. **UNIT.** Sentence scope, fenced code excluded, headings carried as context.

**Sentence scope is where the precision comes from.** Merit prose puts three to six sentences on a line, so a line-scoped filter reports one candidate for a paragraph in which one clause is a forcing constraint and five are not, and cannot say which.

**26 of 30, which is 86.7 percent.** Version 1 was tuned against a 12-claim eyeball read at seed 7; the definition was then **frozen**, and the measurement sample was drawn from the frozen definition at seed `20260908`, uniformly over the whole population, **28.6 percent of it**. Each of the 30 was read against its own file. **The four false positives are named in the entry with the reason each failed**, and the weakest kind (COST, 4 of 105) is kept rather than deleted after the fact, because deleting a kind once you have seen its errors is how a precision figure gets manufactured.
